Moulin Rouge

There are evenings you enjoy, and then there are evenings that quietly move into the part of your memory reserved for stories you’ll still be telling years later. Walking into the Moulin Rouge was one of those.

For months we’d known we were coming. Ever since booking our little hotel just around the corner, that famous red windmill had been peeking out from the end of the street like it was teasing us.

Tonight was finally the night.

After an unhurried cocktail in the little bar opposite, watching the crowds gather beneath the glowing red sails, we wandered across the road and joined… absolutely nobody. At exactly 8:15 the doors opened, we walked straight in without a queue, and suddenly found ourselves stepping into one of the most famous theatres in the world.

Outside, Paris buzzed with scooters, laughter and flashing cameras.

Inside, it was like stepping back into another age.

Deep red carpets. Gold trim everywhere. Soft lighting. Waiters weaving effortlessly between tables dressed in immaculate white. Around 850 people quietly finding their seats, all speaking different languages, yet sharing exactly the same expression.

Expectation.

We settled into our table, ordered a very pleasant bottle of French wine and simply looked around.

“This is going to be special,” I whispered.

Dana smiled.

“I think you’ve got that right.”

Then the lights faded.

And for the next two hours, reality disappeared.

The opening alone was enough to leave the audience speechless.

Sixty women, dressed entirely in elegant white, swept onto the stage alongside twelve men in crisp white suits. Every movement was perfectly synchronised, every step impossibly graceful.

Then, in one perfectly timed moment…

The ladies whisked away part of their costumes.

There was a collective gasp across the theatre.

Not because it was shocking.

Because it was so beautifully choreographed.

The famous Doriss Girls somehow managed to make costumes consisting of little more than feathers, sequins, rhinestones and extraordinary confidence look effortlessly elegant. Nothing felt crude or uncomfortable. It was simply part of the art, celebrating beauty, movement and glamour in a way only the Moulin Rouge seems able to achieve.

I did find my eyes wandering occasionally.

Purely from an appreciation of the choreography, obviously.

Dana gave me one of those sideways looks that has become highly refined over the years.

I returned my attention to the dancing.

Immediately.

Every scene transported us somewhere completely different.

One moment we were wandering through old Paris with cafés, artists and romance.

The next we were watching circus performers dancing.

Then came the knife thrower.

A beautiful blonde assistant stood against the board while knives flew past her at terrifying speed.

I caught myself studying her arms afterwards.

No scars.

No visible signs of previous bad evenings.

Whoever he was…

He’d clearly had an excellent week.

Just when you thought the stage couldn’t possibly surprise you again, the floor slowly opened.

Out of nowhere, an enormous glass-sided water tank rose into view.

One of the dancers dived gracefully into the water and performed what can only be described as an underwater ballet. She twisted, floated and spun beneath the surface with such elegance that you almost forgot she was somehow managing not to breathe.

It was mesmerising.

Then there were the acrobats.

Two men who apparently had no interest whatsoever in gravity.

They balanced on each other’s shoulders, arms and heads with astonishing control before finishing with one performer standing in a headstand… balanced only on the head of the man below him.

No wires.

No visible support.

Then, because that clearly wasn’t difficult enough, the lower performer calmly walked down a flight of steps…

…turned around…

…and walked back up again.

The entire theatre erupted.

By now we’d almost forgotten we were watching a cabaret.

It felt more like watching the Olympics performed by artists.

And then…

The music everyone had been waiting for began.

The Can-Can.

Instantly the atmosphere changed.

The audience burst into applause before the first kick had even landed.

Skirts flew.

Legs seemed to reach impossible heights.

The timing was absolutely flawless.

Behind all the smiles was extraordinary athleticism. Every leap, split, pirouette and high kick demanded the sort of fitness you’d expect from elite athletes rather than dancers wearing thousands of feathers and crystals.

No video could ever do it justice.

No photograph could capture the energy.

It simply had to be experienced.

As the final curtain fell, nobody rushed for the exits.

Around us people simply sat for a moment, almost needing time to process everything they’d just witnessed.

We looked at each other.

“Worth it?” I asked.

Dana laughed.

“I think that’s the quickest answer you’ve ever known.”

Absolutely.

The Moulin Rouge has been entertaining audiences since 1889, surviving wars, changing fashions and over a century of Parisian history. Its current production, Féerie - meaning enchantment or magical spectacle - doesn’t tell one continuous story. Instead, it sweeps you through dreams, old Paris, circus acts, romance and impossible feats of human skill, stitched together with dazzling costumes and breathtaking choreography.

Walking back into the warm Paris night, the famous red windmill still turning above us, the streets somehow felt quieter than before.

Perhaps because for two hours we’d forgotten the outside world existed.

Some attractions become famous because of clever marketing.

Others earn their reputation night after night, performance after performance, for more than 130 years.

The Moulin Rouge belongs firmly in the second category.

And as we wandered back towards our little hotel in Montmartre, still replaying favourite moments from the evening, we both knew we’d just seen something we’ll probably spend the rest of our lives trying to describe…

…and never quite managing to do it justice.
