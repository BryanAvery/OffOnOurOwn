A day that began as a lazy holiday cliché and somehow turned into an unplanned expedition into the beating heart of local life, complete with rainstorms, pearls, and £2 cocktails.

The morning started with a blissful nothingness. We swam, we lounged, we congratulated ourselves on being very good at being on holiday. Then the clouds gathered like they’d been tipped off about our smugness. Within seconds, the sky opened and the beach emptied, leaving us huddled under a palm tree, laughing as warm rain sheeted down our backs. By the time it stopped, we were soaked, suncreamless, and oddly energised.

And so began the “Pearl Mission: Part Two.” The market glittered with trays of perfect spheres in every shade from snow to champagne. Dana, our self-declared pearl inspector, had her game face on. After some serious price comparison and the kind of squinting usually reserved for fine art, she found her pair: luminous, elegant, and, crucially, half the UK price. “They’re perfect,” she said. “And genuine.” Bryan, ever the romantic realist, muttered, “That’s what you said about the fake Louis Vuitton.”

Next up was Highlands Coffee. Bryan, feeling confident with Google Maps, ordered the Grab taxi and proudly announced, “We’re just around the corner.” One mile later, in full tropical sun, we finally arrived. Dana didn’t care. She had her prize — the elusive travel mug she’d been eyeing up all week. The coffee was almost an afterthought.

Then came the fateful sentence: “Look, there’s a local clothes market nearby!” Bryan said, as if it were destiny. Google Maps promised a quick ride. Reality had other ideas.

We were dropped on the wrong side of a bridge, next to a row of fishing stalls where the air smelt of salt, oil, and the faint tang of yesterday’s catch. As we walked, the road narrowed, and soon we were surrounded by the quiet hum of local life — small one-storey houses, paint peeling, with open doors revealing glimpses of families cooking, children laughing, and the occasional rooster strutting like it owned the block. The sound of scooters faded behind us, replaced by the rhythmic splash of someone washing clothes in a bucket.

Dana slowed. “Are you sure this is right?” she asked, her voice light but tight.

“Of course,” Bryan said, though his tone lacked conviction. Google’s blue dot was spinning in circles.

We turned left down a walled alley, the sunlight thinning to a dusty haze. The air was still, heavy, and thick with the smell of drains. Somewhere nearby, a dog barked once — sharp, echoing. A woman paused her sweeping to stare at us, eyes curious but unreadable. Bryan tried a friendly nod; she didn’t return it.

Dana’s hand found his arm. “Bryan,” she said softly, “this is not a market.”

At the end of the alley, there was no market. Just a dead end of houses, laundry lines sagging with damp clothes, and a rusted gate. The tension was a physical thing now, pressing at the back of our necks. Bryan glanced around for a polite exit that didn’t involve trespassing. “Maybe if we—”

“No,” said Dana firmly. “We go forward.”

So we did, navigating another narrow path that looped, twisted, and, mercifully, led us back to the main road. When we finally emerged, blinking into the sunlight and noise of scooters, we both exhaled in perfect synchrony. It felt like escaping a film scene that could’ve gone either way.

Peace was restored with coconut water and a glorious foot massage, the kind where forgiveness is negotiated through kneading pressure points. By the 6,000th step of the day, harmony had returned.

That evening’s reward? The Phu Quoc Night Market — a riot of colour, smells and laughter. We bartered for Crocs (five pairs, naturally), inspected pearls by rubbing them together (the white powder test, very scientific), and settled onto stools roughly six inches tall for cocktails that cost less than a loaf of bread.

Five cocktails later (Bryan’s maths, not Dana’s), standing up became an Olympic event. The world tilted slightly, people bumped into him “by accident,” and he declared the pavement unsteady. Dana wasn’t convinced.

As we staggered back through the glowing chaos of the market, Bryan slurred something about “the authentic side of Vietnam,” and Dana just laughed — that wonderful, melodic laugh that means you’re an idiot, but you’re my idiot.

Tomorrow we might aim for a calmer day. Then again, with us, even buying pearls seems to come with an unexpected adventure.
