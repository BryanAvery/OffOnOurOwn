If Hồ Chí Minh were the father of Vietnam, then Tô Lâm is one of the current caretakers of his legacy, though with rather fewer revolutionary beards and far more government suits.

Tô Lâm, born in 1957, is now the General Secretary of the Communist Party of Vietnam, which, in a one-party country, makes him the most powerful person in the nation. Forget titles like “President” or “Prime Minister”, in Vietnam, the General Secretary is the real boss, the one who guides policy, appoints leaders, and quietly ensures everyone toes the party line.

Before becoming Vietnam’s top man in August 2024, Tô Lâm served as the Minister of Public Security, which basically made him the head of Vietnam’s police and intelligence network. In other words, he’s no stranger to knowing everything that goes on, from political whispers in Hanoi to perhaps even your hotel Wi-Fi history (so best behave).

Alongside him are two other key figures:

  • Lương Cường, the President, mostly a ceremonial role, smiling for photos and greeting visiting dignitaries.
  • Phạm Minh Chính, the Prime Minister, who handles the day-to-day running of the government, budgets, and the economy.

The trio keeps Vietnam humming along as a socialist republic with a strong dose of state control and economic pragmatism. Since the late 1980s, the country has followed a “Đổi Mới” (Renovation) policy, keeping communist politics but embracing capitalism in business. That’s why you’ll find both Lenin statues and Louis Vuitton shops in the same city block.

Under Tô Lâm, the focus is on stability, growth, and security. Vietnam is seen as one of Asia’s most promising economies, attracting foreign investment and tourism while maintaining tight political discipline. Corruption crackdowns have been fierce, though critics say they sometimes conveniently target rivals.

Still, for the average traveller like us, what you’ll notice most is the calm order beneath the chaos. Streets may buzz with mopeds and noodle stalls, but behind it all runs a remarkably efficient system, one that owes much to the quiet hand of Tô Lâm and his Party comrades.

Dana said it reminded her of Prague in the old days, except the beer’s colder here and nobody checks your papers every five minutes. Bryan said the only thing missing was a British politician who actually gets things done.

So while Hồ Chí Minh’s portrait still watches from every note and classroom, Vietnam’s present belongs to a new generation, more polished, more pragmatic, and still very much in charge.
