We arrived at VinWonders Phu Quoc with the enthusiasm of two people who thought they had it all figured out — early start, clear skies, and a full day of thrills ahead. The plan was simple: beat the rain, see the giant turtle aquarium, watch the famous shows, and maybe, just maybe, brave a few rides.

At nine sharp, we strutted through the gates feeling like VIPs. At 9:03, we discovered the rides didn’t actually open until ten. Ah. A small snag in our master plan. So, we shoved our bags into a locker big enough to hide a Vespa and went in search of coffee.

Dana’s came first: a glorious coconut coffee, complete with a scoop of ice cream and a spoon for proper excavation. She ate it like dessert, eyes half-closed in bliss. Bryan’s was the condensed-milk classic, though buried under enough ice to host a penguin convention. Two sips, and the coffee was gone. Still, it was delicious — the kind of sweet, nutty perfection that almost made up for his frozen disappointment.

By ten, the rides were awake and so were we.

Our first mistake was curiosity. We wandered into a dark, echoing building that smelled faintly of oil and adventure. The queue was empty — always a bad sign. A staff member gestured us forward, locking us into seats with our feet dangling. Dana gave Bryan a nervous side-eye. “What is this?” she whispered. “Probably something gentle,” he lied.

Seconds later, the world dropped away.

The car shot forward into pitch darkness with a roar that ripped the air from our lungs. Lights flashed, turns came out of nowhere, and both of us screamed so loudly we startled ourselves. Dana’s scream was high and continuous; Bryan’s was intermittent and full of regret. Somewhere mid-loop, he shouted, “I didn’t sign up for this!” but the rollercoaster, unsympathetic, hurled us onward.

When it finally screeched to a halt, we just sat there, silent, legs shaking, hair wild, staring at each other in stunned disbelief. “Never again,” Dana panted. Bryan nodded — then, naturally, suggested we try the big outdoor one next.

No queue. No mercy.

This one launched like a rocket, flinging us skyward before flipping, twisting, and dragging our stomachs off into another dimension. We went over, under, sideways, upside down — the full washing-machine experience. At one point, Dana screamed so hard she accidentally inhaled air mid-loop and hiccupped the rest of the way. When it finally slowed, Bryan looked pale and glassy-eyed. “That was amazing,” he croaked, “but I think my spleen’s still up there.”

To calm our nerves (and possibly find our organs), we wandered over to the water park. Smaller than the one earlier in the week, but still promising lazy-river serenity. We floated along for a few peaceful minutes before heading to the slides.

Then came the incident.

The lifeguard eyed Bryan up and down with professional seriousness, shook his head, and pointed to a sign: 100kg maximum. Bryan smiled innocently. “Sorry, no English,” he said, waving vaguely toward the stairs. The man switched to hand gestures — big belly, crossed arms. Dana was doubled over laughing. Bryan kept nodding and pretending confusion, inching closer to the slide. Finally, the lifeguard sighed and said it plainly: “Too… big.”

That ended negotiations. Bryan retreated with wounded dignity, muttering something about faulty scales.

We regrouped inside the giant turtle aquarium, where Dana marvelled at glowing jellyfish and elegant stingrays. Bryan admired the engineering but stayed slightly uneasy — he’s always happier seeing sea life in the wild. “They’re beautiful,” he said softly, “but I still prefer the real ocean.” Dana smiled, spooning the last of her ice cream. “Not everyone can dive with sharks, you know.”

The mermaid show came next, an underwater ballet of glitter and grace performed in the aquarium itself. The crowd was packed, grateful to be dry, and as the swimmers twirled through columns of bubbles, even Bryan was impressed. “Alright,” he admitted, “that was magic.”

As dusk fell, we joined the crowd for the grand light show, a spectacle of lasers, fountains, and booming music that looked like a rave for sea gods. Ten minutes in, the sky opened. Rain poured. Everyone scattered, squealing and laughing as we made a mad dash for the exit.

We dove into a Grab taxi, drenched but grinning like fools. Dana looked at Bryan, mascara streaked, and said, “You screamed louder than I did.”

Bryan shook his head, mock-offended. “I was just… expressing appreciation.”

Moral of the story: never trust a dark building, always respect the lifeguard, and remember — courage is just fear after coffee.
