Our final morning on Phu Quoc began as all great goodbyes should: slowly, lazily, and with one last smug dip in the sea. The beach was calm, the waves polite, and even the sand seemed to whisper, stay a bit longer. Bryan finished Swimmingly, his book about marathon swims, and there, right at the end, it mentioned him and SwimTayka alongside the great swimmers of our time. He sat there blinking at the page like a man who’d just discovered his name carved on the Mount Rushmore of aquatics. “I’m in print!” he said, chest puffed. Dana, ever the realist, smiled. “Yes, darling. You and Michael Phelps. Practically twins.”

Then came the day’s main event: the full Vietnamese body massage — our farewell gift to ourselves before flying home.

The spa was dimly lit, thick with the scent of lemongrass and eucalyptus, a kind of fragrant warning that something intense was about to happen. Two women appeared, each barely five foot tall, but built entirely of sinew and confidence. “Relax,” they said, smiling. In hindsight, this was not advice, but a dare.

They began with the feet. Oh, the feet. Tiny fingers pressed into every tendon with military precision, thumbs digging into the arches like they were trying to discover oil. Bryan’s toes cracked like popcorn; Dana’s masseuse was rolling her ankles in slow circles, pulling each toe until it gave up with a small, alarming snap. It should have hurt, but somehow it was glorious.

Then came the legs — long, slow strokes that turned into sharp, surgical attacks on muscles that had clearly done something to offend them. Dana winced, half laughing, half crying, as her masseuse kneaded her calves like bread dough. Bryan, meanwhile, was having what could only be described as a religious experience. “Oh my god,” he whispered, “she’s found muscles I didn’t know existed.”

The women moved up to the arms next, pinching, rolling, twisting each finger, elbow and wrist until our limbs felt entirely detachable. Dana’s arms flopped about like spaghetti; Bryan’s made small involuntary jerks every time his masseuse hit what she confidently called “energy points” — or, as Bryan called them, “pain factories.”

Then the backs. Good lord, the backs. They climbed up onto the tables with the grace of cats and the determination of builders. One moment we were lying there in serenity, the next we were human climbing frames. Knees pressed into hips, palms pushed deep between shoulder blades, elbows slid along the spine. It was both violent and tender, like being disciplined by an angel.

When the cracking began, it was magnificent. Pop, pop, pop — a symphony of joints re-entering society. Bryan’s masseuse worked methodically up his spine, each click more dramatic than the last, until even Dana’s therapist stopped to applaud the final crescendo.

Just when we thought it was over, they attacked the shoulders and neck. Dana’s head was gently twisted until her neck gave a delicate crack, prompting a giggle from her masseuse. “New woman now,” she said proudly. Bryan’s masseuse performed something resembling a judo throw that ended with him making a noise that can only be written as hnnnf.

And then, as if to apologise for all that brutality, they finished with the scalp. Ten delicate fingers danced through our hair, slow and rhythmic, pressing temples, tugging lightly, soothing every nerve they’d just set on fire. By the end, we were puddles. Two blissed-out puddles on two white sheets.

We both mumbled “Ôi trời ơi” (“Oh my God”) in unison, which made them burst out laughing. Apparently our pronunciation was closer to “Oh big sky,” but the feeling was mutual.

Wobbling out of the room, half an inch shorter and ten years younger, we agreed it was the best massage of our lives. Bryan swore his soul had been re-aligned. Dana swore she no longer had bones.

Lunch was at a no-frills Phở Gà café where the soup was heavenly, the price laughable (£3 for both), and the fan above us held together with the kind of faith usually reserved for religion.

Then one final detour—back to the pearl market. Bryan, allegedly just browsing, had clearly fallen in love with a bracelet. Dana gave him the look. “You realise you’re turning into me?” He smiled sheepishly. “Yes, but with better taste.”

Soon, we were at Phu Quoc Airport, surrounded by cranes and scaffolding—proof the island was still in metamorphosis, stretching its wings to become the next big tropical darling.

As the plane took off, the blue shimmer of the Gulf of Thailand beneath us, Bryan sighed. “We’ve done it all—chaos, calm, cocktails, karaoke, and chiropractic warfare.”

Dana smiled, resting her head on his shoulder. “Yes,” she said softly, “and somehow, we’re still in one piece.”

Goodbye, Vietnam. You bent us, bruised us, and made us blissfully happy.
